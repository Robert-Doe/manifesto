Module 21 — Autopsy I: Honey & Grammarly

What two of the most-installed extensions actually do with their extraordinary permissions.

The Core Lesson

Both Honey and Grammarly are legitimate products with legitimate use cases. Neither is malware. But both make choices that prioritize business model over user interests — and those choices are only visible if you know how to read a manifest.json and understand what <all_urls> actually means.

Honey: Replaces affiliate links. Legal, disclosed on page 47 of ToS. Ethically questionable. Builds a purchase history profile for PayPal.

Grammarly: Reads every text field on every website. Necessary for the product. Creates an enormous attack surface — 10M users' keystrokes through one company's infrastructure.

How NeuralTab Is Different

NeuralTab also uses <all_urls>. The difference: all data stays in IndexedDB on your device. No network requests from content scripts. No server receives your history. The autopsy.html page shows the comparison table — use it as your reference when evaluating any extension.

Reading a Manifest Like a Security Researcher

Before installing any extension, check: permissions requested, host_permissions scope, web_accessible_resources count, and background script network activity. Five minutes of inspection reveals more than reading the marketing description.
